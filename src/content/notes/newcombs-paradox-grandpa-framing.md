Assume that as a child you had a very close and loving relationship with your
grandpa. You spent a lot of time together and he got to know you very well
until you were 14, when, sadly, he passed away. He was an excellent
psychologist with a keen intuition for people's personalities and choices. You
know of 25 cases in which he made predictions about family members' important
decisions, and he was right in 20 of them (i.e. 80%). He was also quite
wealthy and had an _interesting_ sense of humor.

![A smiling grandfather in a cardigan plays chess with a young boy at a kitchen
table, watching the boy rather than the
board.](../../assets/notes/newcombs-paradox-grandpa-framing/grandpa-chess.jpg)

About four years later, on your 18th birthday, you get a letter from your
grandpa's lawyer who informs you that before passing away he wanted to leave
you a legacy. For this purpose he personally locked two metal boxes inside the
vault of a Swiss bank. The first box is labelled A and the second B. When you
enter the vault you can see both locked boxes. You **can't** see the contents
of box A, but box B has a reinforced glass window so you can see the money
inside. A bank employee will unlock and hand you either box A or both box A and
box B, depending on your choice (if you insist you could also choose only B,
but it will hardly seem sensible). In fact, you are allowed to stay in the vault
as long as you want to consider your decision. Nobody will be in there with you
until you call the employee to announce your choice. The bank is under strict
instructions to destroy what remains of the boxes, including their content, as
soon as you exit the vault. Your grandpa tried to predict whether you
would choose only box A or both box A and box B.

![A young man stands alone in a bank vault, hand on his chin, looking at two
locked metal boxes labelled A and B on a
table.](../../assets/notes/newcombs-paradox-grandpa-framing/vault.jpg)

According to the will:

- If his prediction was that you would open only box A then he placed
  $1,000,000 in box A as well as $20,000 in box B.
- If his prediction was that you would open both boxes, then he placed **only**
  $20,000 in box B and left box A empty.

In other words, box B always contains $20,000 and box A may be empty or contain
$1,000,000.

Nobody knows what the prediction was and nobody has so far looked into the
boxes. There is no time travel, no magic tricks, no helpers who quickly switch
the content of the boxes and your grandpa is really dead. The boxes are already
filled with money or not and there are no tricks to change that after you make
your decision.

What do you do? Do you walk out with only box A, leaving the $20,000 in box B
_you can actually see_ on the table? Or do you walk out with both boxes?

## Background

In [Can you control the
past?](https://joecarlsmith.com/2021/08/27/can-you-control-the-past/) Joe
Carlsmith briefly sketches this framing for Newcomb's Paradox.

> I find that my two-boxing intuition strengthens if Omega is your great
> grandfather, long dead (h/t Amanda Askell for suggesting this framing to me
> years ago), and if we specify that he’s merely a “pretty good” predictor; one
> who is right, say, 80% of the time (EDT still says to one-box, in this case).
> Suppose that he left the boxes in the attic of your family estate, for you to
> open on your 18th birthday. At the appointed time, you climb the dusty
> staircase; you brush the cobwebs off the antique boxes; you see the thousand
> through the glass. Are you really supposed to just leave it there, sitting in
> the attic? What sort of rationality is that?

I like this framing so I spelled it out above to use in the future when
explaining Newcomb's Paradox, since I find that some people bounce off the
_near-perfect Omega_ framing.
